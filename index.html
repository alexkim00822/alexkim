Independent research

Telling pills apart

2025 to present · Early development mentored by a biostatistician at Johns Hopkins Hospital · Carried out with the Bergen County Youth Assembly’s Active Bergen (Health and Safety) group

I built a Python model that sorts pills by shape, color and size, trained on 8,784 reference photos of 4,231 different medicines from the National Institutes of Health. The goal is a tool that helps older adults and people with low vision notice when two of their medicines look alike.

I compared two approaches. The first measures each pill with OpenCV (how round the outline is, its average color, its width in pixels) and hands those numbers to a Random Forest; it reached 93% accuracy on shape and 85% on color. The second retrains a ResNet18 neural network in PyTorch on the same photos; it reached 95% on shape and 89% on color. Both models make the same mistakes, such as calling a capsule an oval or a red pill pink, which says more about where the NIH labels blur together than about the models.

The data also showed why the tool matters. Of the 4,089 medicines with full labels, 98.5% share their shape, color and size (within one millimeter) with at least one other medicine, and the typical pill has 60 such look-alikes. Round white pills about 6 mm across alone cover 183 different medicines. Shape, color and size cannot identify a pill; only the imprint can. So the finished tool does not name a medicine. It compares two pills and rates the risk of mixing them up as high, medium or low, so they can be stored and labeled separately.

  • OpenCV + Random Forest
  • ResNet18, PyTorch
  • 8,784 NIH photos
  • 4,231 medicines
